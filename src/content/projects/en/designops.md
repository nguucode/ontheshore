I joined as the only designer, and a year later I was leading a team of about eight. What got us there was not hiring fast. It was treating the team itself as something to design, with Nielsen Norman Group's [DesignOps 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-operations-101/) as the map. NN/g describes DesignOps as orchestrating and optimizing people, process and craft so design's value holds up at scale. In practice, for me, it meant one question asked over and over: what is stopping designers from designing?

To show what that looks like day to day, I built a [live demo](https://designops.ontheshore.biz): an illustrative seven-person team, one Lead, two Seniors and four Juniors, mapped onto all 27 items, with who owns each one, how often it runs and a sample artefact. The people and numbers in it are made up; the way of working is the one described here.

## When one designer is the whole department

If you have been the only designer in a product company, you know the feeling. Every squad needs you this sprint, every request arrives through a different channel, and most of your week goes to meetings, handoffs and chasing feedback. NN/g lists exactly this as the first reason DesignOps exists: designers end up too busy to design.

Hiring more people does not fix that on its own. Without a shared way of working, two designers simply produce two styles, and five produce five. I wanted the team to grow without the work splitting into as many directions as there were people.

## I treated the team as a design problem

NN/g's advice for starting is the same advice you would give for any product: research first. Before I proposed anything, I talked to the people around design, the product managers, engineers and leadership, about where work with design got stuck.
The other thing that helped was reading the framework as a menu, not a checklist. It has three areas and nine components, and NN/g says plainly that no organization needs all of it at once. So I put the components on a simple Now, Next, Later roadmap and let the biggest pain point decide what came first.

## How we work together

This is the area most teams skip. In NN/g's 2020 survey of 557 practitioners, it was the weakest of the three, and only 11% had a documented career ladder ([source](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/designops-maturity-low/)). I started here because people decide whether everything else sticks.

**Organize.** I chose a hybrid structure. Each designer sits with a product squad and knows that product deeply, but everyone belongs to one design practice with one way of working. NN/g's comparison of [team models](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-team-models/) names the risk of a purely embedded team: nobody owns process improvement. In a hybrid team, that owner was me.

**Collaborate.** Designers spread across squads drift apart quickly, so the team needed fixed moments together.

**Humanize.** Hiring, onboarding and growth had to be the same for everyone, not dependent on who happened to interview you.

## How we get our work done

**Standardize.** One design process from brief to handoff, written down, with a clear reason for each activity in it, and one toolset everyone actually uses.

**Harmonize.** Shared knowledge, so nobody starts from a blank page: a design system for components and tokens, and one place where research and decisions can be found again.

**Prioritize.** With designers in different squads, I needed to see capacity across the whole team to know who could take what, and to say no with a reason rather than a feeling.

## How our work creates impact

**Measure.** We agreed on what "good" and "done" mean for a design before it goes to engineering, and used design principles as the yardstick in reviews, so feedback stopped being a matter of taste.

**Socialize.** Design only gets a seat at the table if people can see what it changed, so we shared results and success stories with the rest of the company on a regular rhythm.

**Enable.** Some design work does not need a designer. Teaching product managers and engineers the basics kept the team from becoming a bottleneck.

## What I would do differently

The impact area is where I fell short. I built clear ways to measure and reward good work inside the design team, but I did not build them for the product team as a whole. A designer recognised by the design team was not necessarily recognised by product or by the company, and that gap matters to the people on the team more than any ritual. If I did it again, I would agree on one shared way of evaluating outcomes with product and engineering from the start, so that what design rewards is also what the company rewards.
